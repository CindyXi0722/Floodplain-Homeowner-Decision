How Homeowner's Decision Impact Disaster Recovery

A homeowner living in a floodplain has two main ways to protect themselves: buy flood insurance (pay a premium every year, and the insurer pays for most of the damage after a flood), or raise the house (pay a large one-time construction bill to lift the home so floodwater flows underneath it). Insurance doesn't stop the water, but it pays for the repairs so the family can rebuild. Raising the house stops most of the damage from happening in the first place.

A yearly premium can't be compared fairly with a one-time construction bill, so this page adds up the total expected cost over 10 years for each choice, including the flood damage the homeowner would still have to pay for out of pocket. Move the sliders to see which choice costs less, and when.

What to notice when you move the sliders

  • The flood sliders work together. Damage depends on how often it floods times how bad each flood is. A 10% yearly chance of 1 ft of water costs far less than a 10% chance of 6 ft. Watch the gray "do nothing" line: it only climbs steeply when both sliders are high.
  • Raising the house only "turns on" for deep water. With the house lifted ~6 ft, a 3 ft flood passes underneath and causes no damage, so the orange line stays flat no matter how often it floods. Push the depth past about 5 ft and the orange line starts to rise, because the water now reaches the raised floor.
  • Insurance shifts the loss; it doesn't remove it. With a fixed premium, the blue line barely changes when you move the flood sliders. The insurer pays the repair bill, and the homeowner pays only the deductible. Check the box to let the insurer price the risk, and the premium jumps as floods get more likely or deeper. That's how real risk-based pricing works.
  • Time matters. Insurance is cheap at first and grows every year. Raising the house is expensive on day one and almost free afterward. In 10 years, insurance usually wins at moderate risk. Raise the flood chance or depth, or turn on risk pricing, and the break-even year moves earlier.
  • Recovery. Look at the row "damage from one flood, paid by the homeowner." Without protection, one flood can cost a family tens of thousands of dollars at once, often more than their savings. Either option turns that into a manageable, predictable cost, and that is what makes recovery after a disaster possible.

Where the numbers come from

  • Insurance premium ($300–$6,000 per year, default $1,800). The average National Flood Insurance Program (NFIP) policy costs about $786 per year nationally (2023 data, Kiplinger), but homes inside high-risk flood zones pay more. Under FEMA's Risk Rating 2.0 (since 2021–2022), each home's premium is based on its own flood risk, and full-risk premiums in high-risk zones commonly reach several thousand dollars per year.
  • Cost to raise the house ($20,000–$200,000, default $60,000). Typical costs to lift a house run about $25,000–$100,000, with a national average near $50,000 for a 1,500 sq ft wood-frame home raised 6 ft, or roughly $14–$60 per sq ft including the new foundation (Fixr). Larger, brick or slab homes can cost well over $100,000 (Texas Tribune, after Hurricane Harvey).
  • Flood chance (0.5%–10% per year, default 2%). FEMA's high-risk "Special Flood Hazard Area" is land with at least a 1% chance of flooding each year (the "100-year flood"). FEMA notes this is about a 26% chance over a 30-year mortgage. Homes closer to a river or coast can flood much more often.
  • Damage vs. depth. The percent of the house destroyed at each water depth comes from the U.S. Army Corps of Engineers depth-damage curve for a one-story house without a basement (USACE Economic Guidance Memorandum 04-01). For example, 1 ft of water ruins about 23% of the house's value, 3 ft about 40%, and 6 ft about 59%.
  • Other assumptions. House rebuild value $250,000 (the NFIP's maximum building coverage, so the policy covers the full house); insurance deductible $2,000 (NFIP offers $1,000–$10,000); raising lifts the floor 6 ft; risk-based premium = expected yearly claim × 1.3 to cover the insurer's costs (an illustrative assumption). The model ignores contents, inflation and interest, and counts at most one flood per year.
  • A real-world note. In a high-risk zone, a federally backed mortgage requires flood insurance. Many homeowners who raise their house still keep a policy, which becomes much cheaper once the house is elevated. This page treats the two as separate choices so the trade-off is easy to see.

How the costs are calculated

Let p = yearly flood chance, d = water depth (ft), V = $250,000 house value, D(x) = fraction of the house damaged by x ft of water above the floor (USACE curve), and T = 10 years.

Damage from one flood (house as it is) L = D(d) × V Damage from one flood (raised 6 ft) Lr = D(d − 6) × V
Insurance 10-yr cost = Premium × T + p × T × min(L, Deductible)
Raise house 10-yr cost = Elevation cost + p × T × Lr
Do nothing 10-yr cost = p × T × L
Risk-based premium ≈ 1.3 × p × (L − Deductible) Chance of ≥1 flood in T years = 1 − (1 − p)^T Break-even year = Elevation cost ÷ (yearly cost of insurance − yearly expected damage when raised)
